Why one thing

The problem

The list was never the problem.

Most task apps assume the hard part is remembering. If you have ADHD, the hard part is usually starting — and a list of twenty-three things is twenty-three chances to start none of them.

You already know what needs doing. The tabs are open, the reminders fired, the notes app has four versions of the same thought. What's missing isn't information. It's a first move.

  • 01

    Everything feels equally loud

    Twelve things arrive at the same volume, so none of them gets picked. From the inside, priority isn't obvious — it's just noise with deadlines attached.

  • 02

    Starting costs more than doing

    Plenty of tasks take four minutes and three weeks to begin. A task written vaguely has no visible first move, so it stays vague, and the cost of starting keeps climbing.

  • 03

    Energy isn't a nice-to-have

    A task you have no fuel for isn't really a task. It's the thing you stare at, feel bad about, and close the app to avoid.

How it works

Three steps. Then it gets out of your way.

  1. 1

    Dump it

    One box. No categories, no due dates, no priority flags, no projects to file things under. Type it the way you'd say it out loud, badly punctuated, in whatever order it falls out.

  2. 2

    It sorts the mess

    The pile comes back as real tasks: a title that says what the thing actually is, an honest time estimate, what it'll cost you in energy, and a first step you could do in two minutes.

  3. 3

    You get one

    A single task on screen. The rest sit behind one collapsed row — parked, out of sight, and still there when you want them.

The one rule

It picks. That's the whole point.

Showing you the full list hands the hardest decision back to the person least able to make it right now. So my.adhd chooses: it weighs what's genuinely urgent, then discounts anything that needs more fuel than you have. Short tasks win ties, because momentum beats optimality.

When it chooses wrong — and it will — you get two honest ways out. Not this one moves on without argument. Too big breaks the task into steps until one of them looks small enough to start.

One task you'll actually begin beats five perfectly ranked ones you won't.

Deliberately missing

No streaks. No points. No guilt.

No XP, badges, streak counters, calendars, sub-projects, tags, or notifications. Every one of those turns the app into homework: another system to maintain, another place to fall behind, another number that resets to zero on a bad week.

There's no account either, and no sync. What you type stays in your browser. Signup is where people leave, and a tool you have to sign up for is a tool you use once.
