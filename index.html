How it works

The problem

A pile is not a list.

Most task apps assume the hard part is remembering. If you have ADHD, the hard part is usually everything arriving at once — work, money, the dentist, the laundry, all at the same volume, none of it shaped into anything you can act on.

You already know what needs doing. It's in open tabs, three half-written reminders, and a notes app with four versions of the same thought. What's missing isn't the information. It's the shape.

  • 01

    It all arrives at once

    A tax deadline and a load of laundry land in the same breath, in the same tone of voice. Sorting them costs energy you were going to spend doing them.

  • 02

    You can't size what you can't see

    An unestimated task feels infinite. Four minutes and three weeks look identical until something puts a number on them — so the four-minute one waits just as long.

  • 03

    Starting costs more than doing

    Beginning a task costs more than finishing it. A task written vaguely has no visible first move, so it stays vague, and the cost of starting keeps climbing.

How it works

Three steps. Then it gets out of your way.

  1. 1

    Dump it

    One box. No categories, no due dates, no priority flags, no projects to file things under. Type it the way you'd say it out loud, badly punctuated, in whatever order it falls out.

  2. 2

    It reads the mess

    Every line comes back as a real task: a title that says what the thing actually is, an honest time estimate, what it'll cost you in energy, how urgent it really is, and a first step you could do in two minutes.

  3. 3

    You get lists

    Everything grouped by what it is — work, money, home, health, social, errands, admin. Urgent things sit at the top of their list. Nothing is hidden, and nothing is thrown away.

The sorting

Read properly, then put in order.

"gym session takes about an hour" becomes a health task of sixty minutes. "file the tax return, deadline is tomorrow" becomes one urgent money task, not two fragments. Half-thoughts get rewritten into actions; pure worry with nothing to do about it gets dropped.

Order carries the same judgement. Inside a list, the most urgent thing comes first, then the shortest — momentum beats optimality. Between lists, whichever holds the most pressing item leads. So the thing that actually matters is the first thing you see, without anything else being hidden from you.

Tap any task for its two-minute first step. If it still looks too big, break it down turns it into steps until one of them looks small enough to start.

A task you can see the size of is a task you can start.

Deliberately missing

No streaks. No points. No guilt.

No XP, badges, streak counters, calendars, sub-projects, tags, or notifications. Every one of those turns the app into homework: another system to maintain, another place to fall behind, another number that resets to zero on a bad week.

There's no account either, and no sync. What you type stays in your browser. Signup is where people leave, and a tool you have to sign up for is a tool you use once.

And a new dump adds to your lists — it never wipes them. Think of something at eleven at night, dump it, and it joins what's already there instead of replacing it.
